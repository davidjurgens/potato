Politeness codebook

Version 2.3 · last revised after the round-2 adjudication meeting · supersedes v2.2

This is the authoritative document for the politeness annotation task. The banner on the annotation screen is a summary of it; where the two disagree, this document wins. Changes since v2.2 are marked [new in 2.3].

1. What you are judging

Rate how the message would land on the person receiving it, assuming that person is a peer — not someone you manage and not someone who manages you. This framing matters because the same sentence reads differently up and down a hierarchy, and a dataset in which annotators silently assumed different power relations is not measuring one construct.

You are not judging:

  • whether the message is effective;
  • whether you would have written it that way;
  • whether the request itself is reasonable;
  • whether the writer is a polite person.

2. The three labels

Polite

The writer does visible work to lower the imposition on the reader. Look for at least one of:

  • Hedging — when you get a chance, if you have a moment, no rush.
  • Gratitude or apology for the ask — thanks so much, sorry to add to your pile.
  • An offer that costs the writer something — happy to take this one off your plate.
  • Explicit release of pressure — whenever, this is not blocking me.

Absence of rudeness is not politeness. A message that simply does not offend is Neutral.

Neutral

The message states its business plainly. No softening, but no pressure cue and nothing that would sting. In our pilot, 61% of real workplace messages landed here, so if you find yourself using Neutral rarely, re-read rule 2.

Impolite

The message would make a reasonable peer wince. The recurring forms:

  • Sarcasm, including sarcastic politeness markers.
  • Blame or accusation — you never told me.
  • Pointed reminders — third time asking.
  • Public callouts — naming someone's failure in a channel rather than a direct message.
  • Deadline as leverage — a deadline invoked to pressure rather than to inform.

3. Rules that settle most disagreements

Rule 1 — Judge the message, not the sender. You can almost always imagine a workplace where a blunt message is normal. Rate the text as written, to a peer.

Rule 2 — Terse is not impolite. This is the single largest source of disagreement in this task: it accounted for 38% of the adjudicated conflicts in round 1. A bare imperative with no pressure cue (“Send me the slides before the meeting.”) is Neutral.

Rule 3 — Politeness markers can be sarcastic. “Thanks so much for finally getting to this” contains a thank-you and is Impolite. Read to the end before labeling.

Rule 4 — Ignore formatting and typos. All-caps, missing punctuation, and spelling errors are not politeness signals here. This is a deliberate scope decision, not an oversight: register and care-in-writing are a separate construct we annotate elsewhere.

Rule 5 — When genuinely torn, choose Neutral. Do not alternate to split the difference. A consistent fallback keeps your disagreements interpretable; alternating makes them noise.

Rule 6 — Emoji do not upgrade a message. [new in 2.3] A demand with a smiley is still a demand. Judge the words; treat emoji as tone-neutral. Added after round 2, where two annotators had been reading a trailing 🙂 as a politeness marker and two had been ignoring it.

5. Edge cases we have already ruled on

CaseRuling
Message is only a link, or only a file. Neutral. There is no politeness work either way.
Message is warm but the request is unreasonable. Rate the wording. An unreasonable ask phrased kindly is Polite.
Message is in a language you do not read fluently. Skip it. Do not guess — a guess here is indistinguishable from a judgment in the exported data.
Message quotes someone else being rude. Rate the writer's own words, not the quotation.
Message is a template or autoreply. Neutral unless the template itself contains one of the Impolite forms.
