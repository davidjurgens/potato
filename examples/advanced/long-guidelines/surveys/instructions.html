Rating politeness in workplace messages

You will see short messages of the kind people send colleagues over email or Slack. For each one, decide how the message would land on the person receiving it — not whether you personally would have written it that way.

The three labels

Label Use when Do not use when
Polite The writer does visible work to soften the imposition: hedges (when you get a chance), thanks, apologies for the ask, or an explicit offer to make things easier. The message is merely inoffensive. Absence of rudeness is Neutral, not Polite.
Neutral The message states its business plainly. No softening, but nothing that would sting either. Most routine work messages land here. There is a clear pressure cue — a deadline used as leverage, a pointed reminder, an accusation.
Impolite The message would make a reasonable colleague wince: sarcasm, blame, a public callout, or a demand with no acknowledgment of the other person's time. The message is only terse. Brevity on its own is Neutral.

Rules that resolve most disagreements

  1. Judge the message, not the sender. You will often be able to imagine a manager for whom “Send me the slides.” is normal. Rate the text as written, to a colleague you do not manage.
  2. Terse is not impolite. This is the single most common source of disagreement in this task. A five-word request with no hedging is Neutral.
  3. Politeness markers can be sarcastic. “Thanks so much for finally getting to this” contains a thank-you and is Impolite. Read the whole message before labeling.
  4. Ignore formatting and typos. All-caps, missing punctuation and spelling errors are not politeness signals here.
  5. When genuinely torn, choose Neutral. Do not split the difference by alternating — a consistent fallback keeps your disagreements interpretable.

Worked examples

Message Label Why
“Hi Sam, could you send me the Q3 report when you get a chance? No rush at all — thanks so much!” Polite Hedged request, explicit release of time pressure, thanks.
“Send me the slides before the meeting.” Neutral Bare imperative, but no pressure cue and nothing that stings. Rule 2.
“This is the third time I’m asking for the report.” Impolite Pointed reminder used as reproach.
“Thanks so much for finally getting to this.” Impolite Thanks + finally reads as sarcasm. Rule 3.
